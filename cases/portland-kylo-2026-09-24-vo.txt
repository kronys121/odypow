On the morning of September 24th, 2026, a German shepherd named Kylo started what was supposed to be his last shift. After about six years with the Portland Police Bureau and 139 captures, he was set to retire. By two o'clock that afternoon, Kylo and the man he was chasing would both be dead. The whole thing, from the first shout to the last shot, took about ninety seconds. And when detectives picked up the gun that started it all, they found something nobody in that parking lot could have known.

Gresham, Oregon, a city on the eastern edge of the Portland metro area. Around seven in the morning, police are sent to a house on Southeast Market Street for a welfare check. Inside, they find the man who lives there: sixty-year-old Jeff Thompson. He's alone, and he's dead. He appears to have been shot in the head.

A multi-agency homicide unit, the East County Major Crimes Team, takes the case. Two things stand out almost immediately. First, Thompson's car is missing from the driveway. Second, a witness tells detectives that the night before, someone was seen at the house, talking with Thompson, and then going inside with him. Surveillance video shows Thompson's car leaving the area a short time later. Detectives now have a car, a description, and a man who may have been the last person to see Jeff Thompson alive.

Detectives enter Thompson's license plate into the automatic license plate reader system as a "plate of interest." Those cameras are mounted on poles, on patrol cars, and in this case, on a private commercial vehicle. That vehicle drives past the plate. The alert puts Thompson's car near the Montavilla Motel, at Southeast 99th and Pine, in Portland. Detectives find the car parked on the street. They watch a man come and go from it. He matches the clothing and description the witness gave.

Here the case takes a strange turn. Going through records, detectives believe they know who this man is: someone with a recent connection to Thompson and an open warrant on a firearms charge. They're wrong about the name. The man is actually thirty-nine-year-old Eddie Brandon Todd Campbell. But by coincidence, Campbell has a warrant too: a violation of his post-prison supervision on a weapons offense.

That warrant gives police a legal reason to pick him up and question him about the murder. The job goes to the U.S. Marshals Service Pacific Northwest Violent Offender Task Force: federal marshals working alongside local officers, including Portland Police. Their job is tracking down high-risk fugitives. Among them is Portland Police K9 officer Jerry Ables, a twenty-one-year veteran. And his partner, Kylo.

At about 1:48 in the afternoon, task force members pull up in two vehicles on the north side of the Montavilla Motel. They make contact with Campbell. He runs almost immediately: west on Pine, across 99th. Two people on the street later tell investigators they saw something silver in his hand. One of them thinks it's a gun.

A law enforcement plane is overhead, and it records what happens next. Near 97th, a woman is driving east on Pine. Campbell runs up to her car and tries to take it. She refuses to get out. Instead, she hits the gas. Campbell ends up in the driver's seat with her as the car speeds east. Then Officer Ables's vehicle turns onto Pine, heading right at them, and blocks the road. The car slows. Campbell jumps out, runs alongside it for a few steps, then turns back and sprints west again.

Think about that driver for a moment. She didn't get out, and that decision may have kept her out of everything that came next.

At 97th, Ables stops and shouts a warning: stop running, or I'm sending the dog. Campbell keeps going. Ables releases Kylo and follows on foot. Campbell turns south on 97th and cuts into the parking lot of the Chestnut Tree Inn, on Southeast Stark Street. Kylo is right behind him.

Coming the other way up 97th is a second task force member: Portland Police Officer Kameron Fender, seventeen years on the job. He sees Campbell and the dog in the lot, gets out, and starts shouting commands.

Kylo catches him. Investigators later conclude the dog most likely grabbed Campbell's backpack, not his body. There were no obvious bite marks at the autopsy. Surveillance video shows Kylo latching onto the backpack and dragging Campbell to the ground.

Look at where everyone is. Campbell is on the ground, back against the building. Kylo is behind him, still holding on. Fender is in front of him. Ables is off to the side. The memo describes it as a triangle that boxes Campbell in. Neither officer has any cover. Two more task force members, a Clackamas County deputy and a deputy U.S. Marshal, are moving in from the street with rifles.

And this is where it all turns. On both officers' body cameras, a silver handgun appears in Campbell's right hand. According to the District Attorney's memo, he holds it with both hands, appearing to point it directly at Officer Fender, before switching it to his left hand. Both officers order him to drop the gun. A man sitting in his car at the other end of the lot hears it too. Campbell doesn't drop it.

Four officers fire, all at almost the same instant. Ables fires four times. Fender, five. The Clackamas deputy, three. The marshal, once. Thirteen rounds. About ten of them hit Campbell.

Kylo, still holding the backpack, is directly behind him.

According to reporting on the footage, after the shots Officer Ables can be heard shouting one word: "No."

Task force members move in, push the gun away and handcuff Campbell. Within a minute, patrol officers arrive and start emergency first aid. Paramedics are on scene in about five minutes. At around 1:59 p.m., Eddie Campbell is pronounced dead.

Kylo is rushed to one animal hospital, then a second. Veterinarians recommend euthanasia. Officers line up to salute him, and his body, draped in an American flag, is escorted to the Oregon Humane Society. According to local news reports, he is the first Portland police dog killed in the line of duty since 2014. It was his last shift. In the days that follow, the bureau confirms what many had already guessed. Kylo was hit by police gunfire.

Then detectives look at Campbell's gun. It's a .22-caliber Jennings, a small, cheap semi-automatic pistol. There's no magazine in it. The chamber is empty.

The magazine turns up on the sidewalk along 97th, the street Campbell ran down seconds before the parking lot. It likely fell out during the chase. One live round is lying on the ground next to him. More are loose in his pants pocket.

In other words, the gun pointed at Officer Fender could not have fired. But nobody in that parking lot knew that, and under the law, that's the question that matters: what the officers could reasonably believe in that moment, not what was found afterwards.

Investigators also run the gun through the national ballistics network. The ATF compares it to shell casings recovered at Jeff Thompson's house in Gresham and reports what it calls a "presumptive significant link." The state crime lab still has to confirm it. Preliminary toxicology finds THC, methamphetamine and alcohol in Campbell's system.

On October 8th, two weeks after the shooting, the Multnomah County District Attorney's Office, led by District Attorney Nathan Vasquez, announces its decision. The use of deadly force by all four officers was justified under Oregon law. No charges. The office doesn't even take the case to a grand jury. With multiple body cameras, aerial video, surveillance footage and independent witnesses, it says there are no material facts in dispute.

The memo also addresses the hardest question head-on: officers fired knowing a police dog was right behind the suspect. Quote: "This remains true even with Kylo being in danger of being hit, as they had no other reasonably viable alternatives available to them given the immediate deadly threat Mr. Campbell presented."

The same day, the Portland Police Bureau publishes its video. And that's where a second argument begins. The bureau's release is an edited video summary with narration and context. Unedited portions of the footage are included in it. City Councilor Sameer Kanal objected publicly. In his words: "Incomplete footage which goes beyond objective documentation of an incident undermines trust in both local law enforcement and the City broadly." Kanal called for a clear written policy on how use-of-force footage is presented to the public.

Police Chief Bob Day pushed back. Releasing raw video without context, he argued, can leave viewers "without important information needed to understand what they are seeing." The bureau says it releases both, so the public can choose.

It's a debate that goes well beyond Portland. When police release their own footage of their own shootings, who decides what the public sees first?

Officers Ables and Fender were placed on standard administrative leave. The shooting still goes to an internal review, the Police Review Board, and a final ruling from the chief on whether it followed bureau policy. The state lab confirmation on the gun is still pending. And because Eddie Campbell is dead, the question of whether he killed Jeff Thompson will never be tested in a courtroom.

What's left is ninety seconds of video, an empty gun, and a dog who was one shift away from retirement.
